Review: Hedge Funds and the Collapse of Long-Term Capital Management

by Franklin R. Edwards

“I once asked a roomful of economists — all of whom were gathered to discuss household personal finance — to answer, on the fly, the consumption-smoothing question for a hypothetical forty-year-old. I gave them the pertinent information and watched their faces. They weren’t happy. They knew they were about to produce a very wrong answer. Right then and there, they seemed to realize that even financially literate people like themselves couldn’t make proper financial decisions via introspection.” (p. 6)

Introduction

For my review of the book Money Magic by Laurence Kotlikoff I stumbled upon an very interesting happening in the financial markets at the end of the 90s, which until then was completely unknown to me: The downfall of the hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management. Through my investigation of the Merton-Samuelson-Theorem, frequently used in the book, I red about the life of Robert C. Merton, who (together with Myron S. Scholes) received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1997 for their “[…] pioneering formula for the valuation of stock options.”1 Both nobel laureates served on the board of directors for the hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management - founded in 1994. After very successfull years for the hedge fund, and less than a year after the nobel comittee wrote, that Merton and Scholes research “[…] has also generated new types of financial instruments and facilitated more efficient risk management in society.” LTCM had be bailed out by the who-is-who of the financial operators at that time. I was hooked. How could a hedge fund led by two economist, from which many thought “They should know better.”, collapse like this? Luckily, I found an article written by Franklin R. Edwards and published 1999 in the Journal of Economic Perspectives2, which does not only provide conclusive insights in the happening, but also is in my opinion well written. This reviews tries to sum up the content of the paper.

Short Summary of the Content

Published in 2022, the book provides a comprehensive look at lifetime financial planning and addresses many important questions, such as: How do I choose a lucrative career? How do I obtain a quality education affordably? Until when does it currently make sense to work? Is it financially beneficial to marry - or to divorce? And much more. Prof. Kotlikoff includes many “What would be best for Max Mustermann in situation Y given X?”-style examples. As an economist, I particularly enjoyed the many calculations that effectively support his points.

My Thoughts on the Article

The book was definitely worth its purchase price (about €12 on Amazon3) as well as other opportunity costs. Besides the many interesting aspects I would recommend to everyone, two ideas truly made me think: the concept of the Living Standard Cone and the logic behind “Retirees should invest more in stocks the older they get.”

The Living Standard Cone

My Take-Away

The ideas discussed above capture only a small part of what fascinated me about this book and what I genuinely took value from. If I had to summarize my biggest insight, it would be this: Perhaps I should increase the share of safe assets in my portfolio. Even though I am not a retiree yet (and hopefully won’t be for a long time - I enjoy me working!), every additional euro I save slightly reduces the relative importance of my regular income. From the perspective of living standard paths and the Merton-Samuelson theory, it may therefore be time to allocate a larger portion of my wealth to truly safe assets.
