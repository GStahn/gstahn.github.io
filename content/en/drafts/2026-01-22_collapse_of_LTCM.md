# Introduction

For my review of the book [Money Magic by Laurence Kotlikoff](/content/en/post/reviews/2025-11-25_moneymagic.md) I stumbled upon an very interesting happening in the financial markets at the end of the 90s, which until then was completely unknown to me: The downfall of the hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management. Through my investigation of the Merton-Samuelson-Theorem, frequently used in the book, I red about the life of [Robert C. Merton](https://mitsloan.mit.edu/faculty/directory/robert-c-merton), who (together with [Myron S. Scholes](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/faculty/myron-s-scholes#experience)) received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1997 for their "[...] pioneering formula for the valuation of stock options."[^1] Both nobel laureates served on the board of directors for the hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management - founded in 1994. After very successfull years for the hedge fund, and less than a year after the nobel comittee wrote, that Merton and Scholes research "[...] has also generated new types of financial instruments and facilitated **more efficient risk management in society**." LTCM had be bailed out by the who-is-who of the financial operators at that time. I was hooked. How could a hedge fund led by two economist, from which many thought "They should know better.", collapse like this? Luckily, I found an article written by [Franklin R. Edwards](<https://capital-markets.law.columbia.edu/people/franklin-r-edwards>) and published 1999 in the Journal of Economic Perspectives[^2], which does not only provide conclusive insights in the happening, but also is in my opinion well written. This reviews tries to sum up the content of the paper.

# Short Summary of the Content

Published in 2022, the book provides a comprehensive look at lifetime financial planning and addresses many important questions, such as: How do I choose a lucrative career? How do I obtain a quality education affordably? Until when does it currently make sense to work? Is it financially beneficial to marry - or to divorce? And much more. Prof. Kotlikoff includes many “What would be best for Max Mustermann in situation Y given X?”-style examples. As an economist, I particularly enjoyed the many calculations that effectively support his points.

# My Thoughts on the Article

The book was definitely worth its purchase price (about €12 on [Amazon](https://www.amazon.de/-/en/Money-Magic-Economists-Secrets-Better/dp/0316435996/ref=tmm_pap_swatch_0)[^3]) as well as other opportunity costs. Besides the many interesting aspects I would recommend to everyone, two ideas truly made me think: the **concept of the Living Standard Cone** and the logic behind **“Retirees should invest more in stocks the older they get.”**

# My Take-Away

The ideas discussed above capture only a small part of what fascinated me about this book and what I genuinely took value from. If I had to summarize my biggest insight, it would be this: Perhaps I should increase the share of safe assets in my portfolio. Even though I am not a retiree yet (and hopefully won’t be for a long time - I enjoy me working!), every additional euro I save slightly reduces the relative importance of my regular income. From the perspective of living standard paths and the Merton-Samuelson theory, it may therefore be time to allocate a larger portion of my wealth to truly safe assets.
